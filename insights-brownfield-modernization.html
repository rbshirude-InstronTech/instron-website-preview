Insights · Industrial Automation

Modernize a running plant without starting again.

Most automation projects happen in plants that are already producing. The hard part is not the new control system. It is changing it around production schedules, legacy equipment and the people who run the line.

Instron Technologies engineering teamNovember 20266 min read

Why brownfield is harder than greenfield

In a new plant, the control system is designed together with everything else. In a running plant it has to fit around what is already there: PLC generations that no longer talk to each other, OEM packages with closed controls, changes made over the years that were never documented, and a production schedule that leaves short windows for change.

The risk sits in the gaps: an interlock nobody wrote down, a network dependency that appears on start-up day, or an operator workaround that was quietly holding the process together.

What waiting costs

Separate control islands make batches harder to repeat and records harder to keep. Manual batching and fixed-speed equipment limit capacity, consistency and traceability. Reports are compiled by hand, spares for obsolete hardware get harder to find, and more of the plant’s know-how ends up in a few people’s heads.

A five-step approach

  1. Audit the installed base

    I/O lists, drawings, PLC and HMI versions, networks, panels, and how operators actually run the process. Mark what works, what is at end of life and what is undocumented.

  2. Decide what to keep

    Reactors, vessels and drives in good condition can usually stay. Automate and connect them, and replace only where the risk or the gain justifies it.

  3. Define the control structure

    For batch plants, an ISA-88 structure separates equipment from recipes, so products and procedures can change without rewriting code, and every batch leaves a record.

  4. Test before site

    Test the new logic against the agreed specification at factory acceptance, then confirm it on site. A URS, FDS, FAT and SAT give every stakeholder the same reference.

  5. Cut over in planned windows

    Stage the change area by area around shutdowns, with fall-back plans, trained operators and support on site through start-up.

When to replace instead

Retrofitting is not always the answer. Replace when hardware is past support and spares have become a production risk, when the process has changed enough that the old control philosophy no longer fits, or when safety or compliance requirements cannot be met on the existing platform. The audit in step one is what lets you make that call on evidence rather than habit.

In short

  • Audit what you have before you decide what to replace.
  • Keep equipment that still works; automate and connect it.
  • Structure batch control so recipes and records are repeatable.
  • Test before site, and cut over in planned windows.

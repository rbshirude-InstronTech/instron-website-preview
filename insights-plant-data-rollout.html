Start with one loss. Plant data people actually use.

Most plant-data projects do not fail on technology. They stall between a promising pilot and the second line. This is the rollout sequence we use with process plants to keep that from happening.

Instron Technologies engineering teamNovember 20266 min read

Why plant-data projects stall

Most plants already collect plenty of data. PLCs, SCADA, utility meters, recipe systems and quality records each hold part of the picture. What is usually missing is operating context: which batch, which line, which shift and which loss a number belongs to.

In our experience, projects stall for four reasons:

  • The goal is “visibility” rather than a specific loss, so nobody can say whether the pilot worked.
  • Dashboards are built for managers, and the people who run the line never use them.
  • Machines and areas from different suppliers report in different ways, so their figures cannot be compared.
  • The plan tries to connect everything at once, and runs out of budget or patience halfway through.

What the gap costs

The cost shows up in hours and in waste. At a California dairy we worked with, pasteurizer trends and utility figures were compiled by hand across more than 12 separate control systems. Compliance reporting took up to two hours a day, and there was little link between utility use and production.

That pattern is common. When data has no context, people rebuild it in spreadsheets, and the effort goes into reporting instead of improving.

A six-step rollout

  1. Name one loss worth money

    Downtime on a critical cell, chemical use per batch, utility use per unit of output, or hours spent on reports. If you cannot put a number on it today, measuring it is the first win.

  2. Prove it on one line or area

    Connect only what that loss needs. Record a baseline first, so the result is a before-and-after in your own figures rather than a vendor estimate.

  3. Bring operators in early

    The people on the line define the downtime reasons, check the numbers and choose the screens they will use. Their trust decides whether the system outlives the pilot.

  4. Write the benefit down

    Turn the pilot result into a short business case: what changed, what it is worth per year and what it took. That is what funds the next phase.

  5. Agree one model before you scale

    Machines and areas never designed to report the same way need a common performance model, rolled up by machine, line and shift. Decide it once, not line by line.

  6. Roll out in phases

    Add areas in order of value. Keep legacy equipment that still works and connect it, rather than replacing it just to make the data easier.

Choosing a partner: three questions

The software matters less than how it is connected to your process. Before you choose a partner, ask:

Can they work with what you have?

Most plants run a mix of PLC generations and OEM packages. A partner should be comfortable connecting them, not only replacing them.

Do they understand the process?

Batch phases, CIP, utilities and quality limits decide what a number means. Process and automation knowledge matter as much as the dashboard.

Who owns the model when they leave?

Tags, downtime reasons and reports should be documented and handed over, so your team can extend them without starting again.

PlantON is our plant-data platform for batch and continuous process plants. We deliver it together with the automation and process work around it, so the data has context from the first day.

In short

  • Pick one loss that costs money, and measure it before you connect anything else.
  • Prove the gain on one line or area, in the plant’s own numbers.
  • Agree one performance model before you scale, so every machine and area reports the same way.
  • Expand in phases, and connect legacy equipment that still works instead of replacing it.

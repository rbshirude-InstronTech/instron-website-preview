Insights · Process Skid Plants

From lab to pilot to industrial. What changes at each step.

A process that works at bench scale still has to prove that it runs, can be controlled and produces data you can scale from. What a skid has to do changes at each stage, and so does what it should cost.

Instron Technologies engineering teamNovember 20267 min read

Three stages, three questions

Around TRL 4 to 6

Pilot

Does the process work outside the lab?

Wide operating ranges, flexible configuration and dense instrumentation, so every run produces data you can compare.

Around TRL 6 to 7

Demonstration

Does it run reliably at a representative scale?

Long runs, recycle streams, maintainability, utilities and operating procedures start to matter as much as the chemistry.

TRL 7 and beyond

Industrial

Does it pay?

Throughput, yield, energy and solvent use, uptime and compliance decide the design, and cost per unit becomes the test.

The balance comes first

Every stage starts from the same document: the mass and energy balance. It fixes flows, compositions, heat duties and utility loads. From those come line sizes, vessel volumes, heat-exchanger areas and pump and chiller duties. Change a feed ratio late and the equipment list changes with it.

For a pilot, the balance also defines the operating window you want to explore. Design for that range rather than a single point, or the pilot cannot answer the questions it was built for.

Data is the real output of a pilot

A pilot exists to produce evidence. Without precise control and data capture, results cannot be compared between runs or used to plan the next scale. That is why instrumentation and automation deserve the same attention as the process equipment.

Design each stage for the next one

  • Build in modules. A methanol-water recovery system in Mexico was delivered in two parts, a process skid and a distillation tower, and led to a second project with the same customer.
  • Keep one data model. Tag names, units and batch records that carry from pilot to demonstration make results directly comparable.
  • Agree test scope per project. Some systems can be fully tested as skids before shipping; others can only be proven after installation. Settle FAT and SAT scope early.
  • Plan the interfaces. Utilities, drains, vents, power and network, and who owns each one at site.
  • Make each stage a decision point. A phased scope lets you stop, change direction or move on with evidence.

In short

  • Each stage answers a different question: does it work, does it run, does it pay.
  • Instrumentation and automation are what turn pilot runs into data you can scale from.
  • Settle the mass and energy balance early. It drives every piece of equipment after it.
  • Design each stage so the next one can reuse its lessons, and sometimes its modules.
